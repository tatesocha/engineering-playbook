Case study 1 · Business problem

Ship on a calendar, not on a mood.

A fixed cadence and a written go/no-go rule turn a judgment call into a check anyone can audit.

The problem, in one line. No cadence and no written threshold. A release went out when someone decided it should.

Why it matters. Releases that depend on one person's mood are hard to trust, hard to hand over, and quietly risky. Every business that ships on a schedule has this decision somewhere: launches, filings, price changes.

A real version of this

Business scenario · illustrative

A regional bank's quarterly rate change

The bank changes card APRs once a quarter. Last cycle, the change went live on a Friday night with one unchecked migration, and the rate table disagreed with the disclosure page for six hours.

What we did

Cadence

Every two sprints

One cut per cycle, at sprint close, so the device walk has a fixed date.

Gate

Seven lines, all checked

Checks pass on the exact commit, the walk is done, migrations are applied, flags are off, notes exist.

Decision

A recorded go

The product lead records go. An ambiguous ok is not a go.

Rules that come with it

  • No silent holds. A blocker is fixed or its reason is written down.
  • Unapplied is not done. A migration not applied is listed under needs a decision.
  • Every row is recorded. The post-cut walk gets a pass or fail per row, even when it passes.

The trade-off, stated plainly

  • Two-sprint cuts mean more changes to walk at once. That is the cost of a fixed walk date.
  • A written gate is slower than a gut call on a quiet day.
  • Simulator walks cannot exercise everything. The list of what was not walked is part of the release.
Why it matters, again. The gate does not make the release faster. It makes the decision repeatable, and it stops a missing sign-off from going unnoticed.

Where else this shows up

  • Any release with a go/no-go call: launches, regulatory filings, price changes.
  • Decision: write the gate lines before the release, and record the go in one place.
  • Owner: the person who makes the call, not the person who is most eager.
